The console is where machines are enrolled, models are enabled, and spend is attributed. You sign in with your company's identity provider — there is no separate console password.

## Workspace

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Dashboard" icon="gauge-high">
    The current state of the fleet, and a setup checklist on a new organization: connect a machine, enable a model, make your first request.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Fleet" icon="server">
    Every enrolled machine, its state, and what it is serving. **Add machine** issues an enrollment token and renders the install command. Selecting a machine opens a detail pane with its telemetry and network path.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Requests" icon="list-check">
    Individual inference calls: model, outcome, latency, tokens, and which machine served each one. The first place to look when something is slow or failing.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Models" icon="box">
    The catalog. Enable or disable a model for your organization, see whether your fleet can actually run it, and watch weights download after you enable one.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Chat" icon="message">
    A playground for trying an enabled model against your own fleet before you write any code. The fastest way to confirm a model works after you enable it.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Usage" icon="chart-column">
    Tokens and cost over time, split between your own hardware and cloud. This is where the value of the fleet shows up as a number.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## The four things worth doing on day one

<Steps>
  <Step title="Enroll two machines, not one">
    One machine is a demo. Two is a fleet — you find out immediately whether placement, failover and the mesh work in your network. See [Enroll a machine](/enroll-a-machine).
  </Step>
  <Step title="Enable one model and wait for it to download">
    The first request after enabling returns `model_warming_up` while machines fetch the weights. Doing this before you point an application at Kibbu means your first real request is a fast one.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Decide about cloud fallback">
    Under **Settings**, either configure a provider — so requests your fleet cannot serve still succeed — or deliberately leave it off, so they fail loudly instead of costing money. Both are valid; drifting into one by accident is not.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Set a budget">
    A spend budget turns a runaway job into a `402` instead of an invoice. Responses carry `X-Kibbu-Budget-Warning` before the cap is reached.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Reading the Usage view

The split between local and cloud is the number that matters. Cloud usage is not failure — it is the ladder working — but a **rising** share means the local rung is failing to serve more often than it used to. The usual causes, in order of likelihood:

1. A newly enabled model is too large for the machines you have.
2. Machines that used to be idle are now in use, or on battery.
3. Traffic grew and the fleet did not.

**Requests** narrows it down: filter to cloud-served calls and look at what they have in common.
